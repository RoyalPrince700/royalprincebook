# Chapter 7: Think Wild — Build, Build, Build

> "I cannot continue like this." — The sentence that changed everything for me, long before I ever wrote a line of code.

You have read six chapters. You know what web development is. You know the MERN stack. You know how to prompt Cursor. You know fonts, colours, navbar anatomy, and you have copy-paste prompts sitting in Chapter 6 waiting for you.

So let me ask you one question, and I need you to answer it honestly:

**Have you built anything yet?**

Not "have you read." Not "have you watched." Not "have you saved the prompts for later." Have you opened a folder, pasted a prompt, run `npm run dev`, and seen *your* project load in the browser?

If the answer is no, this chapter is for you. If the answer is yes but you stopped after one small page, this chapter is also for you.

Because everything I have taught you so far is preparation. **Building is where you actually learn.** Reading this book will not make you a developer. Cursor will not make you a developer. **Projects make you a developer.** Build. Build. Build.

---

## Stop Thinking Small

One of the biggest traps I see beginners fall into is limiting their imagination before they even start.

They think: *"Let me just build a simple landing page first."* Then they build it, feel proud for two days, and never touch code again.

Or they think: *"I am not ready for something big yet."* So they stay in tutorial mode forever — collecting certificates, bookmarking videos, reading threads — but never shipping anything that matters to them.

Let me tell you something I learned leading over 40,000 students and building multiple platforms from scratch: **your mindset sets your ceiling before your skills do.**

When I was sixteen, sitting in a single room with my mother and sister, I did not say, *"Let me just survive."* I said, **"I have to do all it takes to be successful."** I did not know the full path. I did not have the resources. But I refused to think small about my future.

The same applies to what you build.

Do not limit yourself to a five-section landing page if what you actually want is a bookstore, a booking platform, a school portal, or a tool that solves a problem in your community. **Think wild.** Ask big questions. Dream the project you actually want to exist — then break it into steps and build it piece by piece, the same way I built Royal Prince Hub.

| Small mindset | Wild mindset |
|---------------|--------------|
| "I'll copy a tutorial someday" | "I'll start tonight with Step 0" |
| "I need to know everything first" | "I'll learn what each step requires" |
| "My idea is too big" | "My idea is big — so I'll build it in phases" |
| "I'll wait until I have a better laptop" | "I'll use what I have and upgrade as I earn" |

You are not building to impress me. You are building to **become** the person who can build anything they want. That person does not think small.

## Knowing the Tools Is Not Enough

Chapter 2 gave you the toolkit. Chapter 3 taught you how to talk to AI. Chapter 4 and 5 taught you how websites look and work. Chapter 6 handed you the prompts.

That is a lot of knowledge. But knowledge without projects is like owning a toolbox and never building a house.

I learned this the hard way — and the disciplined way.

When I was Student Union President at the University of Ilorin, my schedule was packed. Meetings. Pressure. Responsibilities for over 40,000 students. Final year project on my desk. Most people would have said, *"I am too busy to learn something new."*

I did not say that.

I wanted to learn web development. I picked up coding tutorials — some were 23 hours long. It took me two or three months to finish a single course because I could only study in small windows between duties. But **I kept showing up.** Every day. After my tenure ended, I ramped up to nine hours a day learning to code.

I was not reading about JavaScript for fun. I was building toward something. The tutorials were fuel. **The real growth happened when I applied it — when I started building projects.**

You can watch every MERN stack video on YouTube. You can ask ChatGPT to explain React one hundred times. You will still freeze the first time you open an empty folder unless you have trained yourself by **building.**

**Reading is not enough. Watching is not enough. Bookmarking Chapter 6 is not enough.**

Build.

## In Building Is Where You Know

There is a difference between *knowing about* code and *knowing* code.

You know about authentication when you read that JWT stands for JSON Web Token.

You **know** authentication when you break a login flow at midnight, paste the error into Cursor, fix the middleware, refresh the browser, and finally see your name in the navbar.

You know about payments when you read "verify on the server."

You **know** payments when Flutterwave returns a test transaction and your MongoDB order document updates to `paid`.

That kind of knowing only comes from building.

When I was learning to solve a Rubik's Cube, I watched tutorials for an hour and could solve it — slowly. But to solve it in seconds, I practiced five hours a day for nine months. Boring. Repetitive. No applause. **But that is where mastery lived.**

Web development is the same. The boredom, the errors, the "why is this button not working" moments — that is not punishment. That is the tuition you pay to actually know.

Every project teaches you something no chapter can:

| What you build | What you truly learn |
|----------------|---------------------|
| Landing page | Layout, responsiveness, deploying something live |
| Multi-page site | Routing, shared components, structure |
| Store with cart | State, forms, user flows |
| Full e-commerce | Auth, API, database, payments, admin |

You do not need permission to start at the level that excites you. Pick a track from Chapter 6 — or design your own project — and **learn by building it.**

## Ask Questions. Build Anything.

Cursor and AI have removed the excuse that "nobody is there to help you."

At 2 AM when your API returns a 500 error, Cursor is there. When you forget how React Router works, AI is there. When you are stuck on styling, you can paste your component and ask for a fix.

**Use that.**

Ask questions without shame. Ask the same question twice if you need to. Ask wild questions:

- *"How do I build a booking system for a barber shop?"*
- *"How do I add WhatsApp order notifications to my store?"*
- *"How do I let users upload profile pictures?"*
- *"How do I build a dashboard that shows sales charts?"*

There is no project too ambitious if you break it into steps — the same way Chapter 6 breaks e-commerce into phases. The AI co-pilot helps you execute. **But you have to start.**

Do not wait until you feel ready. I was not ready when I contested for student leadership. I was not ready when I performed spoken word for the first time and failed the audition. I was not ready when I started coding during my presidency. **I started anyway.**

Build the thing you actually want to build. Not the thing that feels safe. Not the thing that impresses nobody. **Your** idea. Your community's problem. Your portfolio. Your business. Your vision.

Think wild. Then build one step at a time.

## No Excuses — Find a Way

Now I need to talk to you directly, the same way I talk about discipline in *Leading from Within*, because some of you are reading this chapter looking for a reason to delay.

Let me remove the common ones:

**"I don't have a good laptop."** Start with what you have. I started learning in cramped windows of time on whatever device was available. Upgrade when you earn from a project or freelance work.

**"I don't have money for Cursor."** Cursor has free tiers. Save for a subscription if you need Pro. Work late. Do side jobs. Sell a skill. Cut unnecessary spending. I grew up in a single room where forty-five thousand Naira a month had to stretch across a family — I know what "no money" feels like. **Find a way.** Buy data if you have to. The cost of staying stuck is higher than the cost of investing in your growth.

**"I don't have fast internet."** Download what you can. Work offline on structure and logic. Use mobile data strategically. Build locally first; deploy when you can.

**"I don't have time."** You have the time you prioritise. I coded while leading a student union. I practiced drums while walking long distances. If you want it, you will carve out 30–60 minutes — even if that means after midnight when the house is quiet.

**"I'll start next week."** Next week becomes next month. Next month becomes never. **Start today.** One folder. One prompt. One step.

You cannot negotiate daily with excuses and expect to become a builder. That is not harsh — that is the truth I lived.

When I decided I could not continue living with limitations, I did not wait for perfect conditions. I worked. I sacrificed comfort. I deleted distractions. I showed up when I did not feel like it.

**You must do the same.**

Work. Work. Work hard. Find all means. Buy the data. Stay up late if that is what it takes. Save for the tools. Build in the morning before class. Build after work. Build on weekends.

Nobody is coming to hand you a developer career on a platter. **Build it yourself.**

## What "Keep Building" Looks Like in Practice

Motivation without a system fades in three days. Here is how you turn this chapter into a lifestyle:

### 1. Always have an active project

The moment you finish one build, start the next. Even if the next one is small. Idle gaps kill momentum.

### 2. Keep a project list

Write down every idea — wild ones included. Pick one as "active," one as "next." Review the list every Sunday.

### 3. Ship something every month

A deployed landing page counts. A working cart counts. A blog with three posts counts. **Shipping** beats perfect planning.

### 4. Log your builds

Create `BUILD_LOG.md` in every project folder. Date. Step completed. Error you fixed. Lesson learned. Future you will thank present you.

### 5. Compare your work — not to steal, but to learn

When you finish a track from Chapter 6, open the Royal Prince Hub codebase. Compare patterns. Ask Cursor: *"Explain how this file differs from mine and what I should improve."*

### 6. Teach what you build

Explain your project to a friend. Post a screenshot. Write a short README. Teaching forces clarity — and attracts opportunities.

## How This Connects to What Comes Next

You might think: *"Royal Prince, you are telling me to build, but what about deployment, scaling, and going live?"*

Good. That means you are thinking like a builder.

Chapter 8 will cover **what to watch for while vibe coding** — `.env` secrets, auth, and catching AI architecture mistakes. Chapter 9 covers **deployment** — Vercel, Render, environment variables, putting your project on the internet for the world to see. But deployment assumes you have **something built** and **built safely.** You cannot deploy an empty folder — or one with secrets exposed on GitHub.

So the order is clear:

1. **Think wild** — decide what you want to exist
2. **Build** — use Chapter 6 prompts or your own plan
3. **Keep building** — project after project, no excuses
4. **Build smart** — Chapter 8 (env, auth, review AI output)
5. **Deploy** — Chapter 9
6. **Repeat** — forever

This is not a school exam you pass once. This is a craft you practice for life.
